1  Introduction

Our "helicopter" is a lever arm on a hinge with a small ducted fan at the end. The goal is to make the arm go to an angle we pick and stay there.

Here's how it works. An MPU-6050 sensor on the arm measures acceleration and spin rate. The Pico 2 combines the two readings to figure out the arm's angle. A PID controller then decides how hard to run the fan, 1000 times a second, and sends that out as a PWM signal through an isolated motor driver.

We can change the target angle and the three PID gains from a serial terminal while it runs. A VGA screen plots the angle, the target and the motor command live. Pressing and releasing a button runs the demo: the arm starts hanging straight down, then goes to 0°, +30° at 5 s, −30° at 10 s and back to 0° at 15 s.

With our final gains (Kp = 80, Ki = 0.2, Kd = 12), the arm hit every demo angle, settled in 1–3.6 s, and stayed stable anywhere from −90° up to about +50°.

Angles in this report: 0° means the arm is level, −90° means it's hanging straight down, and positive means above level. (The course's analytical notes measure θ from hanging down instead, so when we borrow their equations, θ = φ + 90°, where φ is our angle.)

2  The Math and Physics

2.1  Measuring the angle

We have two sensors, and each one is good at a different thing.

The accelerometer feels gravity. When the arm is still, the only thing it measures is gravity, so the direction of that reading tells us which way is down, and so how tilted the arm is (Figure 2a). The arm rotates about the sensor's y axis, so gravity shifts between the x and z axes. Since the arm swings through well over 90°, we can't use a small-angle shortcut; we use the full arctangent:

The good part: gravity never goes away, so this angle never drifts. The bad part: the sensor can't tell gravity apart from any other push. Once the fan is running, vibration and the arm's own motion make this reading really noisy (Figure 19).

The gyroscope measures how fast the arm is rotating (°/s). Add up that rate over time and you get an angle that's smooth and ignores vibration. The catch is that every gyro has a small built-in error b, and adding it up over time makes the angle slowly drift away (after t seconds it's off by about b·t). Each step we just add rate × time:

2.2  The complementary filter

So the accelerometer is right in the long run but jumpy, and the gyro is smooth but drifts. The complementary filter uses both. Every 1 ms, it takes the last angle, adds the gyro's change, and then nudges the result 0.1% of the way toward the accelerometer's angle (Figure 3):

Why "complementary"? The two weights add up to 1. For slow changes (slower than about 1 second, the filter's time constant τ) we trust the accelerometer, and for fast changes we trust the gyro. The real angle gets through untouched; only each sensor's own noise gets filtered out.

  • Gyro drift turns into a small fixed offset (about τ·b) instead of growing forever. A gyro error of 1°/s would leave us about 1° off.
  • Our weights. The notes suggest 0.99/0.01. We used 0.999/0.001, which leans harder on the gyro and keeps more fan vibration out, at the cost of a bigger drift offset. With the fan running, the accelerometer readout bounced 2–5° away from the filtered angle while the filtered angle stayed smooth (compare the two readouts in Section 5.2).

Smoothing before atan2. Noise doesn't average out nicely after a nonlinear function like atan2; it shifts the result. So we first smooth the raw ax and az with a simple digital filter that works like an RC circuit (in the code it's a right-shift by 4):

That adds about 16 ms of delay, but only to the accelerometer, which only has 0.1% say in the answer. The gyro path has no delay. That's the whole reason to use a gyro: if we tried to just heavily smooth the accelerometer instead, the delay would land inside the control loop, and delay is what makes feedback loops unstable.

2.3  How the arm moves

The motor speeds up and slows down much faster than the arm swings, so for our purposes the fan speed just follows the voltage. The arm is the slow part, so the arm is what we have to control.

The arm. Three torques act on it: thrust from the fan (about KTω²), gravity pulling the fan's mass down, and friction in the hinge. Thrust minus gravity minus friction equals how fast the arm speeds up:

For small wiggles around one angle, the arm acts like a mass on a spring, where gravity is the spring. That explains a few things we saw in lab:

  • Hanging down, the arm is just a pendulum. It swings back and forth on its own and barely damps out.
  • The spring flips at horizontal. Below level, gravity pulls the arm back toward hanging. Above level, gravity pushes it further away. So above level, the controller is the only thing keeping the arm up.
  • The arm responds differently at different angles. How much one extra duty count moves the arm depends on how fast the fan is already spinning, so it depends on where the arm is. One fixed set of gains can't be perfect everywhere, which is part of why the stable range has limits (Section 5.5).

2.4  PID control

A lookup table from angle to duty wouldn't work: the thrust needed changes with the supply, motor temperature and so on, and a table can't fix a bump. So we use feedback (Figure 5): measure the angle, compare it to the target, and turn the error into a motor command.

  • P (proportional) pushes harder the further the arm is from the target (Figure 6a). On its own it always leaves the arm a bit low: P only outputs thrust when there's error, and holding the arm up needs thrust. Too much P and the arm oscillates.
  • I (integral) adds up the error over time, the area under the curve (Figure 6b). It keeps growing until the error is gone, so it fixes P's "a bit low" problem. Once the arm is settled, P and D are basically zero and the I term is the thing holding the arm up. Too much I makes the arm overshoot and wobble slowly.
  • D (derivative) looks at how fast the error is changing. It brakes fast motion, which calms down overshoot and oscillation.

Getting D from the gyro. The target only changes in jumps, so between jumps the rate of change of the error is just minus the arm's spin rate: de/dt = −ωy. So we plug the gyro reading straight into D, as suggested in lecture. Taking the difference between angle samples would be way noisier: at 1 kHz the angle barely changes from one sample to the next, so you'd mostly be measuring noise. A bonus is that D doesn't spike when the target jumps (no "derivative kick"), because the gyro doesn't know about the target.

2.5  PWM and the motor circuit

PWM means switching the motor fully on and off very fast and changing what fraction of the time it's on. GPIO 4 is channel A of PWM slice 2. Its counter counts to 6000 and starts over:

The motor is way too slow to follow 1 kHz switching, so it just responds to the average voltage. A duty level of 3280 out of 6001 is like giving the motor 2.7 V:

Why only 1 kHz? The 4N35 optoisolator is slow (it takes tens of microseconds to switch), so a much faster PWM would never fully turn the MOSFET on or off. We also use the same counter wrap to trigger our control loop, so the controller runs at exactly the PWM rate without needing a separate timer.

Why the motor needs protection. A motor coil is an inductor, and inductors hate having their current cut off suddenly. The notes' example (1 mH, 1 A, switched off in 10 ns) gives a huge voltage spike:

The 1N4001 diode across the motor gives that current somewhere safe to go. The 0.1 µF ceramic capacitor soaks up high-frequency noise from the motor brushes. And the 4N35 keeps the Pico completely separate from the motor side: they share no ground, and only light crosses between them (Section 3.2).

2.6  Fixed point and I2C

Fixed point. Floating-point math is slow on the Pico, so the ISR does almost everything (except atan2) with fix15: a 32-bit integer where the bottom 16 bits are the fraction (Figure 8). 1.0 is stored as 65,536.

To multiply, do the product in 64 bits and shift right by 16 (in the PID code we shift by 32, which gives a whole-number duty count directly). Constants get rounded (0.001 becomes 65), which makes the gyro step about 0.8% short, but the accelerometer slowly corrects for that. Two of our bugs came from mixing number formats (Section 3.1).

I2C is the two-wire bus the IMU talks on. Devices can only pull the wires low; resistors pull them back high. To read the IMU, the Pico sends the register address and then reads 6 bytes back. At 400 kHz:

So reading the sensor takes about 40% of every 1 ms loop. That's why the rest of the ISR is lean integer math and all the drawing happens on the other core. The "only pull low" design also explains our worst bug: if the Pico resets in the middle of a read, the IMU can get stuck holding the data line low, and every transfer after that hangs (Section 3.1).

3.2  Motor driver circuit

As required, the driver is soldered on the course PCB (Figure 9). The motor runs off its own 5 V supply through an IRLB8721 MOSFET with a 12 V gate drive. The Pico never touches the motor side: GPIO 4 lights the LED inside the 4N35 through 330 Ω, and the light switches the 12 V onto the MOSFET gate.

  • 4N35: GPIO 4 high → gate pulled up to 12 V → motor on. GPIO 4 low → the 10 kΩ resistor pulls the gate to ground → motor off. That's the "extra 10 k" the course page asks about: without it the gate would float and the motor could turn on by itself. The 1 MΩ resistor helps the 4N35 switch off faster.
  • 1N4001 diode across the motor catches the voltage spike from Section 2.5.
  • 0.1 µF capacitor across the motor soaks up brush noise.

We first tested the board with the course PWM demo and checked that the fan speed followed the duty cycle, then merged the PWM code into the IMU program (where the n command now sets a manual duty).

3.4  The control loop

Everything time-critical happens in on_pwm_wrap() on core 0, once every millisecond:

  1. Clear the interrupt and toggle GPIO 14 (so we can check the timing on a scope).
  2. Read the IMU.
  3. Smooth ax and az and get the accelerometer angle with atan2.
  4. Update the complementary filter with gyro y.
  5. Check the demo button and step the target if it's time (Section 3.6).
  6. If the loop is on, run PID; if not, reset the I term.
  7. Update the PWM level if it changed.
  8. Every 10th time, save the angle and duty for the screen.

Here's the controller part, straight from imu_demo.c (comments shortened):

angle_error = desired_angle - complementary_angle ;              // + means arm is below target
long long p_term = ((long long)Kp) * ((long long)angle_error) ;
long long d_term = ((long long)Kd) * ((long long)gyro[1]) ;      // de/dt = -gyro y
fix15 i_step = multfix15(Ki, angle_error) ;
int pd = (int)((p_term - d_term) >> 32) ;                         // 15.16 x 15.16 -> integer counts
int u_limit = control + DUTY_SLEW ;                                // overcurrent protection: slew limit
if (u_limit > DUTY_MAX) u_limit = DUTY_MAX ;
if (!(i_step > 0 && pd + fix2int15(i_term) >= u_limit)) i_term += i_step ;  // conditional integration
if (i_term > int2fix15(i_max)) i_term = int2fix15(i_max) ;
if (i_term < 0) i_term = 0 ;
int u = pd + fix2int15(i_term) ;
if (u < 0) u = 0 ;
if (u > u_limit) u = u_limit ;
control = u ;

Positive error means the arm is too low and needs more thrust. D is subtracted because a fast-rising arm should get less thrust, and a fast-falling arm should get more.

3.5  Overcurrent protection and windup

When the target jumps, the P term jumps too (80 × 90° = 7200 counts when the arm is let go from hanging). Slamming a stopped motor from 0 to full voltage pulls a big surge of current, because it isn't spinning yet so nothing limits the current except the coil's resistance. So the final code lets the command go up by at most 20 counts per millisecond. Going down isn't limited, so the controller can still cut thrust instantly.

You can see this ramp in every step in Section 5.2, as a dotted line going up in the duty plot. 300 ms is short compared to the arm's roughly 1 s response, so it barely slows things down.

The ramp creates a new windup problem: while the output is stuck at the ramp limit, the error stays big and I would keep growing. So we only let I grow when the output isn't already at the limit. This is the "clamping" fix from the course PID notes, and it's why i_max could stay at 10000 (above the 6000 max output) and never kick in. I is also kept at 0 or above, since negative I would only delay thrust.

6  Conclusion

Our helicopter did everything the lab asked for. It ran the full 0° / +30° / −30° / 0° demo on one set of gains with no reset, every step settled within 3.6 s, we could change the gains and target from the serial terminal at any time, and the VGA showed the angle, target and motor command live.

The results also matched the theory. The complementary filter kept fan vibration out of the angle. Once settled, the I term equalled the hover duty, like PID theory says it should. And the lopsided settling times and hover duties are what you'd expect from a fan that can only push up and an arm whose weight hangs off-centre.

What we'd change next time:

  • Plot a smoothed copy of the motor command (motor_disp) like the handout asks, instead of the raw one.
  • Measure the gyro's built-in error at start-up (average a second of readings with the arm still) and subtract it.
  • Add a "feed-forward" guess of the hover thrust using the r cos φ + d sin φ model, so I only has to fix what's left. That should shorten the slow tails, especially after going down.
  • Move the printf for button presses out of the ISR, since printing inside the 1 kHz interrupt can make one loop run long.
  • Measure the arm's length and mass so we can check the model with real numbers.

Q3. How you selected the three PID gains

Ballpark from units. We picked starting values so each term would give a reasonable duty count:

  • Kp: a 90° error (hanging, target level) should ask for about full power, so Kp ≈ 6000/90 ≈ 67. So: tens.
  • Kd: the gyro rate in °/s is about the same size as the error in degrees, so Kd is also tens. (The course page's "thousands" assumes taking differences of noisy samples, not a gyro.)
  • Ki: I adds Ki·e a thousand times a second, so Ki has to be much smaller. We started near 1/32.

Tuning steps (the order from lecture and the PID notes):

  1. P only: turn up Kp until you find the edge between "wobbles die out" and "wobbles grow." Kp = 120 oscillated steadily at about 1.2 Hz (Figure 18), so we backed off to 80, about two-thirds of that.
  2. Add D until the wobbling stops. Our test: push the arm down below the target and let go. It should spring back, overshoot once, and settle quickly. We ended at Kd = 12.
  3. Add I to get rid of the leftover error and bring the arm onto the target. More I gets there faster but overshoots and wobbles slowly. We settled on Ki = 0.2. At that rate a 5° error grows I by 1000 counts per second, enough to build the roughly 3300-count hover command in a few seconds.
  4. Run the whole demo with the button and adjust. The windup fix (Section 3.5) handled the rest, so we left i_max at 10000.

The live I-term readout on the VGA was our most useful tuning tool: once the arm settles, it should equal the duty, which tells you P and D are done and only I is holding the arm up (Figure 14–Figure 17).

Q4. What are the maximum/minimum angles that produce stable behaviour for your PID gains?

Max ≈ +50°, min −90° (0° = level). The max depended on which 5 V motor supply we used. Why there's a limit:

  • Min: −90° is just hanging. Near there the thrust needed drops to zero, the output sits at 0 and gravity holds the arm, so it's stable for free.
  • Max: above the arm's tipping point (level for a simple arm, a bit higher for ours, Section 5.4), gravity pushes the arm further away instead of back. The higher you go, the stronger that push, until D can't keep up.
  • The plant changes with angle: how much the arm responds to one duty count depends on fan speed, and our gains were tuned for the demo range near level.
  • The supply matters: a different supply changes thrust per duty count (thrust ∝ V²), which scales the whole loop. The same gains end up closer to or further from the edge, so the max angle moved.

B.18  Tuning PID for fast response 2026-10-22 · 20 prompt words · 189 response words

Our prompt (verbatim)
If we want the arm to be very fast response to angle changes, how should we tune the pid parameters
Claude's response (Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Code)

Speed comes from Kp, and Kd is what lets Kp go high without bouncing. Aim for slight overshoot (about 5–10%), which settles faster than a fully damped response. Procedure, using step tests (e.g. target 0 → 30°): raise Kp until it overshoots, raise Kd until the overshoot is damped, and repeat. Stop when either (a) the duty slams between 0 and DUTY_MAX during a step, which means the actuator is saturated and more gain won't help, or (b) the fan chatters at rest, which means Kd is amplifying gyro noise (a low-pass filter on gyro[1] would help).
